How neurons communicate

Nerve impulse and synaptic transmission

Everything you think, feel and do is electrical and chemical signals moving through a network like this one, right now.

Your brain has roughly 86 billion neurons. Each one connects to thousands of others, making somewhere around 100 trillion connections (synapses).

Neurons communicate in two ways: electrically inside a cell (the nerve impulse) and chemically between cells (synaptic transmission). This presentation follows one message from start to finish, zooming from the whole brain down to single molecules and back out again.

What is a nerve impulse?

A nerve impulse (an action potential) is a quick electrical spike that travels along a neuron. At rest, the inside of the cell sits at about −70 mV.

Push it past about −55 mV and it always fires a full spike; fall short and nothing happens. This is the all-or-nothing rule.

membrane potential−70 mV
At rest: the inside of the cell is negative.

At rest, a neuron is like a small charged battery. The inside is about −70 millivolts compared with the outside.

This comes from an uneven spread of ions. There is more sodium (Na⁺) outside and more potassium (K⁺) inside. Potassium slowly leaks out through open channels, taking positive charge with it, so the inside ends up negative. The sodium–potassium pump keeps the balance by pushing 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ it brings in.

When inputs push the voltage up to the threshold (about −55 mV), voltage-gated sodium channels snap open. Na⁺ floods in and the inside briefly turns positive (about +30 to +40 mV). Then the sodium channels shut, potassium channels open, K⁺ flows out, and the voltage falls back, dipping slightly below rest before it settles.

All-or-nothing: a spike is always the same size. A stronger stimulus does not make a bigger spike; it makes more spikes, more often. Right after a spike there is a short refractory period when the neuron cannot fire again.

Try it: press Weak stimulus (no spike), then Strong stimulus (spike). Now press Weak twice quickly: the small bumps can add up and cross threshold. This adding-up is called summation.

A real spike lasts only about 1–2 milliseconds. Here it is slowed right down so you can watch it.

How the impulse travels

Each patch of membrane triggers the next, like falling dominoes, so the spike only moves forward. Myelin insulates the axon, so the signal jumps between tiny gaps called nodes of Ranvier and travels much faster.

Unmyelinated

Slowed down and not to scale. Real speeds: roughly 1 m/s without myelin, up to about 100 m/s with it.

An impulse does not flow like electricity in a copper wire. Each patch of membrane fires and triggers the patch next to it, like a line of falling dominoes. Patches that have just fired need a moment to reset (the refractory period), so the impulse can only travel forward.

The gates that do this are voltage-gated channels: they open when the voltage around them changes, letting ions through.

Myelin is a fatty insulating wrap made by glial cells (oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord, Schwann cells in the rest of the body). The impulse only has to be regenerated at the small bare gaps between myelin segments, the nodes of Ranvier, so it seems to jump from gap to gap. This is saltatory conduction, from the Latin saltare, "to jump".

Without myelin, signals travel at roughly 1 metre per second. With thick myelin they can reach around 100 metres per second.

Neuropsychology link: in multiple sclerosis (MS), the immune system damages myelin in the brain and spinal cord. Signals slow down or get lost, which can cause weakness, numbness and problems with vision.

What is a synapse?

A synapse is where one neuron passes its message to the next cell: another neuron, a muscle or a gland. The two cells never touch. Between them is a gap about 20 nanometres wide, the synaptic cleft.

A synapse has three parts: the presynaptic terminal (the end of the sending neuron's axon), the synaptic cleft (the gap), and the postsynaptic membrane (the receiving side, usually on a dendrite of the next neuron).

The gap is tiny, about 20–40 nanometres. A human hair is tens of thousands of nanometres wide. In this model the gap is drawn much wider so you can see inside it.

The electrical impulse cannot jump across this gap, so the message is turned into chemicals: neurotransmitters, stored in tiny bubbles called vesicles.

A side note: a smaller number of synapses are electrical (gap junctions), where two cells are directly linked. Most synapses in the brain are chemical.

Synaptic transmission

The electrical signal becomes a chemical message, then becomes electrical again on the other side.

Step 1 of 6
The impulse arrives at the axon terminal.

1. The impulse arrives at the axon terminal.

2. Calcium channels open. The change in voltage opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium ions (Ca²⁺) rush into the terminal.

3. Release. Calcium is the trigger: it makes vesicles fuse with the membrane and pour their neurotransmitter into the cleft. This is called exocytosis.

4. Crossing. The molecules drift across the gap in well under a millisecond.

5. Binding. They bind to receptors on the next neuron. This opens channels or starts chemical signals that make that neuron more likely, or less likely, to fire.

6. Clean-up. The signal has to be switched off, or it would keep going. Neurotransmitter is pumped back into the sending neuron (reuptake), broken down by enzymes, or drifts away.

Psychology link: many drugs act on step 6. SSRIs, a common type of antidepressant, block the reuptake of serotonin, so it stays in the gap for longer.

The chemical messengers

Neurotransmitters carry the message across the gap. Some push the next neuron toward firing (excitatory); others hold it back (inhibitory). Tap a molecule to meet it.

Pick a messenger

Each one fits its own receptors. What it does depends on where it is released and which receptor it meets.

Neurotransmitters are the chemical messengers released at synapses. More than 100 different chemical messengers have been identified in the nervous system.

Excitatory messages make the next neuron more likely to fire. Inhibitory messages make it less likely. A neuron adds up all its excitatory and inhibitory inputs and only fires if the total reaches threshold.

Glutamate is the brain's main excitatory messenger and GABA the main inhibitory one. For others, like dopamine, serotonin and acetylcholine, the effect depends on which receptor they bind to. Acetylcholine, for example, excites skeletal muscle but slows the heart.

Too much or too little of a messenger is linked to many conditions: dopamine loss in Parkinson's disease, loss of acetylcholine neurons in Alzheimer's disease, and serotonin in depression (it is targeted by many antidepressants, although its exact role in depression is still debated).

The drawings are simplified chemical structures. You do not need to learn them; they show that each messenger has its own shape.

Say it simplySome messengers say go, some say stop, and the neuron adds them up.

Receptors: the locks

A receptor responds only to a molecule of the right shape, like a lock and its key. Fast receptors are channels that open at once; slow receptors start a chain reaction inside the cell.

A fast receptor is a channel with a lock on top. Send a key to open it.

A receptor is a protein on the receiving cell. It has a binding site shaped for one particular neurotransmitter, like a lock that fits one key. Dopamine will not open a GABA receptor.

Fast (ionotropic) receptors are themselves ion channels. When the neurotransmitter binds, the channel opens within about a millisecond and ions flow straight through. Example: the acetylcholine receptors on your muscles.

Slow (metabotropic) receptors are not channels. When activated, they switch on a G-protein inside the cell, which starts a chain of chemical reactions. The effects start more slowly (tens of milliseconds to seconds) but last longer and can change how the cell behaves. Most dopamine and serotonin receptors work this way.

Psychology link: many drugs are "fake keys". Agonists fit the lock and switch it on (nicotine activates acetylcholine receptors). Antagonists fit the lock but jam it (many antipsychotic drugs block dopamine receptors).

Try it: send the right key, then the wrong key. Switch between fast and slow receptors and compare.

Say it simplyThe message only works if the key fits the lock.

From signal to movement

At the neuromuscular junction, a motor neuron releases acetylcholine onto a muscle fibre. The fibre fires its own impulse and contracts.

The neuromuscular junction is a special synapse between a motor neuron and a muscle fibre.

The impulse reaches the motor neuron's terminal. Acetylcholine (ACh) is released and binds to receptors on the muscle's surface, the motor end plate. The muscle fibre then fires its own impulse, which releases calcium inside the fibre and makes it contract. An enzyme, acetylcholinesterase, quickly breaks down the ACh so the muscle can relax again.

How your brain moves your hand: you decide to move. The motor cortex sends the command down through the brainstem and spinal cord. Most of these fibres cross to the other side in the brainstem, which is why the left side of the brain moves the right hand. In the spinal cord the command passes to a motor neuron, which carries it out along your arm to the neuromuscular junction. The muscle contracts, pulls on a tendon, and your finger bends. Fun fact: most of the muscles that bend your fingers are in your forearm, not your hand.

Clinical links: in myasthenia gravis, the immune system attacks ACh receptors, causing muscle weakness. Botox works by blocking ACh release. Curare, an arrow poison, blocks ACh receptors and causes paralysis.

Try it: press Fire the motor neuron and follow the signal all the way to the finger.

Say it simplyBrain decides, nerve carries, acetylcholine delivers, muscle moves.

Case study: mapping a fly's brain

Scientists have now mapped every neuron and every synapse of a fruit fly, whose brain is smaller than a poppy seed. Pick a year to watch the map grow.

139,255neurons

Within a day of the 2026 male map going public, a student hooked it up to a Minecraft fly, and others tried Doom. It is a simulation of the wiring with game rules added on top, not a living or conscious fly.

A connectome is a complete wiring diagram: every neuron and every synapse in a nervous system.

How it was made: a fly brain was cut into thousands of ultra-thin slices (more than 7,000 for the 2024 map) and photographed with an electron microscope, about 21 million images in all. AI traced each neuron from slice to slice, and scientists plus volunteers around the world checked and corrected the tracing.

2020: the hemibrain (Janelia and Google), a large part of an adult fly's central brain, roughly 25,000 neurons.

2023: a fly larva's whole brain: 3,016 neurons and about half a million synapses.

2024: FlyWire, led by Princeton with more than 200 researchers: a whole adult (female) fly brain, about 140,000 neurons and more than 50 million synapses, published in Nature.

2026: whole nervous systems, brain plus nerve cord. BANC (Harvard and Princeton, Nature, June) mapped a female fly and found that movement is run largely by local circuits near each leg, not by one command centre. MaleCNS (Janelia, Google Research, MRC LMB and Cambridge, Cell, September) mapped a male fly: more than 166,000 neurons and about 125 million synapses, the largest map by neuron count so far. For the first time, male and female wiring could be compared: most of it matches, and the differences cluster in a small set of neurons behind courtship and aggression.

Just for fun: the data is free for anyone to use. Within a day of MaleCNS's release, a Georgia Tech student ran it inside Minecraft, and others tried Doom and Beat Saber. These projects turn game events into inputs, simulate activity on the wiring, and use it to choose pre-programmed movements. The map has no real chemistry in it, so this is a model, not a living or conscious fly.

What it teaches us: synapses are real, countable structures. Knowing the wiring does not tell you everything (you also need to know which chemicals each synapse uses and how strong it is), but it narrows the possibilities enormously.

Say it simplyWe have gone from guessing a brain's wiring to having the complete map.

Why this matters

From a fly's roughly 166,000 neurons to your roughly 86 billion, every brain runs on the same two ideas: electrical impulses and chemical synapses.

The same two ideas run every nervous system we know: electrical impulses inside cells and chemical transmission between them. A fly has roughly 166,000 neurons; you have roughly 86 billion, about half a million times more.

A tested playbook. The slicing, imaging, AI tracing and crowd proofreading worked, and are now being scaled up toward mouse brains.

Understanding disease. Roughly three-quarters of human disease-linked genes have a version in flies, so fly circuits are used to study conditions such as Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease.

Brain-inspired technology. Real wiring data can inspire brain-like (neuromorphic) computer chips, better AI, and future brain–computer interfaces.

A healthy caution: a wiring map is not a mind. It shows who can talk to whom, not what they are saying at any moment.

Neuropsychology perspective: for more than a century, neuropsychology worked backwards, inferring how the brain works from damage (like patient H.M.) or from blurry scans. With a complete connectome, scientists can start from the wiring and ask what behaviour it produces.
